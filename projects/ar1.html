Detecting Stability Near the Unit-Root Boundary

An independent simulation study examining how reliably statistical inference can distinguish stable AR(1) processes from unit-root behavior, especially when the autoregressive coefficient lies very close to the stability boundary.

Python Statistics Time Series ADF Test Monte Carlo Simulation

Research Question

How reliably can we detect changes in the stability of a time-series system, and how does statistical inference behave under different conditions?

The project focuses on AR(1) processes near the unit-root boundary, where a process may technically be stable but still behave very similarly to a unit-root process.

The AR(1) Model

Xt = βXt−1 + εt

The coefficient β controls the persistence of the process. When |β| < 1, the process is stationary. When β = 1, the process contains a unit root.

Method

I used Monte Carlo simulation together with the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test to study both Type I error and statistical power.

The main experiment compared β = 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, and 0.99 across sample sizes T = 50, 100, 500, and 1000, with 10,000 simulations for each condition.

A separate experiment examined β = 0.99 in greater detail across additional sample sizes in order to understand how slowly power improves when the process lies very close to the unit-root boundary.

Key Results

5.9%

Estimated Type I error under the unit-root null, close to the nominal 5% significance level.

95%

Approximate power for β = 0.95 at T = 500.

12%

Approximate power for β = 0.99 at T = 500.

31%

Approximate power for β = 0.99 even at T = 1000.

A process can be technically stable, with β < 1, yet still be extremely difficult to distinguish statistically from a unit-root process when β lies very close to 1.

Interpretation

Statistical power generally increased with sample size, as expected. However, the simulations showed that proximity to the unit-root boundary matters substantially.

When β = 0.99, even large samples often provided limited power. This illustrates that the reliability of inference depends not only on the amount of available data, but also on how close the underlying system lies to the stability boundary.
